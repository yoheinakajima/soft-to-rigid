Searches for dense packings of congruent shapes move rigid pieces. Hardening starts each piece as its inscribed disk and rounds it into the real shape while the container is compressed. We ask whether it is a search worth running alongside existing ones, and test it at matched budget and comparable tuning against rigid starts, growth, simulated annealing and perturbation--compression on seven families of squares, triangles, hexagons and disks in squares, triangles and circles (\CsevenInstances{} instances), scoring each method by the lowest container size it reaches. Hardening is not a better default: tuned rigid starts reach the lowest size most often. It is a different search. On squares in a square it reaches packings the other four methods miss, including Trump's $n=11$ and Wainwright's $n=19$, and at ten times the budget it is the only one of the five to reach the best known packing for $n=\LongHardenOnlyReachedNs$, while annealing alone reaches $n=\LongSaOnlyReachedNs$. Compressing disks and then switching to polygons at once, or growing rigid polygons along hardening's area schedule, shows no detectable difference from rigid starts, and hardening's unique packings contain more tilted pieces. The bias costs elsewhere: hardening is worse on triangles and on four families held out from all development, where choosing the path from a short pilot does no better than always starting rigid. Shape continuation is a search to keep beside rigid ones, because the two reach different packings, not a replacement; we did not show that splitting a fixed budget between them pays. Every experiment was planned before it ran, every run is replayable, and every number is generated from an event log.
