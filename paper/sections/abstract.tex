Most searches for dense packings of congruent shapes move rigid pieces. Hardening instead starts every piece as the disk inscribed in it and deforms it into the real shape under inward pressure on the container; this found a new packing of 12 unit cubes in a cube. We ask whether the path a piece takes through shape space matters in general. Across seven families of squares, triangles, hexagons and circles in squares, triangles and circles (\CsevenInstances{} instances, \totalruns{} runs), we compare five paths at equal budget and equal tuning effort, judging each by the lowest container size it reaches after exact tightening. No path is best everywhere: tuned rigid starts reach the lowest size most often overall, hardening reaches it on almost every instance of squares in a square, including packings no other method found, and rigid or growing starts are better for triangles. Hardening gains more than rigid starts from longer schedules, and at equal total budget, running two paths solves about as many instances as doubling the runs of the best one. Every run is replayable and every decision was planned before the experiment it governs.
