Most searches for dense packings of congruent shapes move rigid pieces. Hardening instead starts every piece as the disk inscribed in it and deforms it into the real shape along a spheropolygon path while the container is compressed; in earlier work this found a new packing of 12 unit cubes in a cube. We ask whether the path a piece takes through shape space matters in general. On seven families of squares, triangles, hexagons and disks in squares, triangles and circles (\CsevenInstances{} instances, \totalruns{} runs), we compare three paths and two classical baselines at matched budget and comparable tuning effort, judging each by the lowest container size it reaches. No path is best everywhere. Tuned rigid starts reach the lowest size most often overall, by a margin close to the resampling uncertainty; hardening reaches it on almost every instance of squares in a square, including two classical packings no other method found in 32 runs, and is worse than rigid starts on triangles. Two ablations locate the effect: compressing disks and then switching to polygons at once, or growing rigid polygons along hardening's area schedule, show no detectable difference from rigid starts, which suggests that the gradual rounding itself helps squares and hurts triangles; on squares in a square, however, the pre-registered test does not separate hardening from the disk-start ablation, so by our own rule the advantage there is attributed to the disk-compressed start. An earlier indication that hardening gains more from longer runs did not replicate in a confound-free repeat. Every experiment was planned before it ran, every run is replayable, and every number in the paper is generated from an event log.
