Most searches for dense packings of congruent shapes move rigid pieces. Hardening instead starts every piece as its inscribed disk and rounds it into the real shape while the container is compressed. In early, untuned comparisons it looked better than rigid starts. We test whether the path a piece takes through shape space matters, on seven families of squares, triangles, hexagons and disks in squares, triangles and circles (\CsevenInstances{} instances), comparing three paths and two classical baselines at matched budget and comparable tuning, by the lowest container size each reaches in 32 runs. Tuned, the picture reverses: rigid starts reach the lowest size most often. Hardening reaches it on \CsevenSquInSquHardenLowest{} of \CsevenSquInSquN{} instances of squares in a square, alone on \CsevenSquInSquHardenSole{} of them, including Trump's $n=11$ and Wainwright's $n=19$ packings, and is worse than rigid starts on triangles. Compressing disks and then switching to polygons at once, or growing rigid polygons along hardening's area schedule, shows no detectable difference from rigid starts; hardening's square wins end with more tilted pieces than rigid starts' best packings. On four families held out from all development, hardening is worse than rigid starts (\DecHLower{} against \DecHRLower{} instances lower), and choosing the path from a short pilot is no better than always starting rigid. The path through shape space is a factor in what a search finds, not a better method; the one family where hardening helps is squares in a square. An earlier indication that hardening gains more from longer runs did not replicate. Every experiment was planned before it ran, every run is replayable, and every number is generated from an event log.
