\section{What we learned}\label{sec:learned}
\paragraph{The path matters, and which path depends on the family.} For squares in a square, hardening's lowest size is the lowest of all methods on \CsevenSquInSquHardenLowest{} of \CsevenSquInSquN{} instances and the only one that low on \CsevenSquInSquHardenSole{}; with identical settings, rigid starts manage \CsevenSquInSquRigidLowest{}. In 32 runs each, hardening alone reaches Trump's $n=11$ and Wainwright's $n=19$ packings (Figure~\ref{fig:strip}). For triangles the order reverses: rigid starts are lowest on \CsevenTriInSquRigidLowest{} instances of triangles in a square against \CsevenTriInSquHardenLowest{} for hardening, and \CsevenTriInTriRigidLowest{} against \CsevenTriInTriHardenLowest{} in a triangle (Figure~\ref{fig:tristrip}). On the control family the gradient paths reach the lowest size on the same number of instances, as they should.

\paragraph{Separating the disk start from the gradual path.} Hardening differs from rigid starts in two ways: pieces are compressed as disks, and they then harden gradually. Campaign C11 separates the two with \emph{snap}, which compresses disks exactly as hardening does and then switches to the polygons in one step, with the same seeds, settings and budget (Table~\ref{tab:ablate}). Its pre-registered test compares, per family, the instances that exactly one of harden and snap solves. On squares in a square only two instances are discordant, so the test is inconclusive ($p=0.5$). The plan's decision rule then attributed hardening's advantage there to the disk-compressed start. That rule was wrong: failing to detect a difference does not show that there is none, still less which mechanism is responsible. We record the rule and its output, and draw no causal conclusion from it.

Exploratory comparisons of the sizes reached, which were not the pre-registered test, are as follows. We detect no difference between snap and rigid starts (each lower on \SnapRXLower{} and \SnapRRLower{} of \CsevenInstances{} instances). Hardening and snap differ in opposite directions by family: counting instances where hardening is lower against those where snap is lower, squares in a square give \SnapSquSqu{}, triangles in a triangle \SnapTriTri{} and triangles in a square \SnapTriSqu{} (all families, \SnapHLower{} against \SnapXLower, $p=\SnapP$). A gradual path from the same start therefore changes which packings are found; a confirmatory test of the direction on squares in a square would need many more discordant instances than these 28 provide.

Campaign C12 asks the same of area. A hardening piece also grows, by up to 40\% of its area; \emph{grow-area} keeps rigid polygons but scales them so their area follows hardening's schedule exactly. Again we detect no difference from rigid starts (lower on \AreaRXLower{} and \AreaRRLower{} instances), and hardening differs from it in the same directions as from snap: \AreaSquSqu{} on squares in a square and \AreaTriTri{} on triangles in a triangle (all families \AreaHLower{} against \AreaXLower, $p=\AreaP$). Its pre-registered test is again inconclusive on squares in a square. Neither the disk start nor the area schedule alone reproduces hardening's pattern of results.

\paragraph{What the pieces do.} Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism} measures three things on the replay of the best run of each instance, for hardening and rigid starts: how far pieces move after compression, how much they turn, and how many end tilted against every container edge. When corners first appear in a hardening run ($\tau=0.5$), the orientational order of squares in a square, $|\langle e^{4i\theta}\rangle|$, has a median of \MechSquSquHardenOrder{}; at the same point of a rigid run it is \MechSquSquRigidOrder, so rigid squares are already aligned while hardening squares are not. On the \MechSoleN{} squares-in-square instances where hardening alone reaches the lowest size, its final packing has more tilted squares than rigid's best on the same instance every time (\MechSoleMore{} of \MechSoleN; median shares \MechSoleTiltH{} against \MechSoleTiltR), as the classical packings at $n=11$, 17 and 19 require. On triangles in a triangle hardening pieces move about twice as far after compression as rigid ones (median \MechTriTriHardenMove{} against \MechTriTriRigidMove{} unit sides), and on the \MechTriFailN{} instances where rigid starts alone reach the lowest size the gap is the same (\MechTriFailMoveH{} against \MechTriFailMoveR). These are descriptive. They fit two hypotheses: that the support function's factor $1-\tau$ lets hardening defer the choice of orientation until neighbours are in place, which squares need when a block must tilt against aligned neighbours; and that triangles, whose corners stick out twice as far as their inradius ($R/\rho=2$, against $\sqrt2$ for a square) and whose good packings alternate up and down, pay for that deferral with large rearrangements. The area explanation alone (a triangle's inscribed disk covers 60\% of it, a square's 79\%) is weakened by hexagons, whose disk covers 91\% and where hardening is no better than rigid starts (\CsevenHexInSquHardenLowest{} against \CsevenHexInSquRigidLowest{} of \CsevenHexInSquN{}, mostly ties). Changing the path also changes contact geometry and forces, so none of this isolates orientation as the cause.

\paragraph{Does choosing the path transfer to new families?} If the better path depends on the family, a search could choose it per instance. Campaign C15 tests that as a decision on four families never used to develop, tune or analyse any method: pentagons and octagons in a square, hexagons in a triangle and triangles in a circle, $n=6$--$20$ (\DecN{} instances). Each instance gets 64 hardening and 64 rigid runs, from which three pre-registered policies of 64 runs are formed: always rigid, always harden, and a pilot that spends 8 runs on each path and the remaining 48 on the path whose pilot went lower (Table~\ref{tab:decision}). The pilot and always-rigid rarely differ (lower on \DecPilotLower{} and \DecRigidLower{} instances, $p=\DecP$), so by the plan's rule we report no detected benefit from choosing the path. Always-harden is worse than always-rigid (\DecHLower{} against \DecHRLower, $p=\DecHP$), most clearly on pentagons, and reaches the best known value on \DecReachedHarden{} instances against \DecReachedRigid{} for rigid starts and \DecReachedPilot{} for the pilot. The pilot picked hardening on \DecPicked{} instances and so avoided most of its losses, but was lower than always-rigid on only \DecPilotLower{} instances. On these families, as on hexagons and triangles before, hardening does not help; the one family where it does is squares in a square.

\paragraph{Partly soft starts.} Varying only the starting softness $\tau_0$ on the \CtauN{} most contested instances (Table~\ref{tab:tau}), the counts of instances where each value reaches the lowest size are \CtauOne, \CtauHalf, \CtauQuarter{} and \CtauZero{} for $\tau_0=1, 0.5, 0.25, 0$. Squares in a square favour soft starts (\CtauSquares{}); triangles favour rigid or slightly rounded ones (\CtauTriangles{}). These instances were selected where hardening and rigid starts disagreed, mostly in rigid's favour, which tilts this count toward small $\tau_0$; partly rounded starts are a candidate default, not an established one.

\paragraph{Longer runs, or more runs?} An earlier test (C09, appendix) suggested that hardening gains more than rigid starts from longer schedules, but it changed run length and restart count together and used instances chosen because the two paths disagreed. Campaign C13 repeats it cleanly on \BudN{} instances fixed in advance ($n=11,17,23,29$ per family; hexagons $n=8,12,16,20$), with 32 runs at three times the budget, and 96 runs at the base budget for the same total cost. Counting instances where hardening is lower against those where rigid starts are lower: \BudOneH{} against \BudOneR{} with 32 base runs ($p=\BudOneP$), \BudThreeH{} against \BudThreeR{} with 32 long runs, and \BudMoreH{} against \BudMoreR{} with 96 base runs. Longer runs improved rigid starts more than hardening (rigid lower than its base on \BudGainR{} instances and higher on \BudLossR; hardening \BudGainH{} and \BudLossH), and at equal cost, three times longer runs and three times as many runs did about equally well for both paths (\BudLvMLH{} against \BudLvMMH{} for hardening, \BudLvMLR{} against \BudLvMMR{} for rigid). The C09 result does not replicate; we withdraw it.

\paragraph{Two paths, or more runs of one?} The paths fail on different families, so running both might help. At equal total budget, 32 rigid runs plus 32 hardening runs solve \PortRigidHarden{} of the \CsevenInstances{} instances, against \PortRigidSixtyFour{} for 64 rigid runs and \PortRigidGrow{} for 32 rigid plus 32 growing runs (instances solved by exactly one of the first two: \PortSign, two-sided $p=\PortP$). We detected no benefit from mixing; at this size that does not show there is none, and whether mixing pays when the family is known to favour the second path was not tested.

\paragraph{Tuning decides comparisons.} Our first experiments compared hardening with rigid starts that inherited the hardening schedule, on two square instances, and hardening looked better. Tuned with comparable effort, rigid starts led overall. Re-tuning for the lowest point instead of the median changed four of the five methods' settings (stronger pressure for the gradient paths, more noise for rigid starts) and helped simulated annealing most. It kept the broad pattern, hardening for squares in a square and rigid or growing starts for triangles, though the leading method changed for squares in a circle and triangles in a square.
