\section{Limitations and open directions}\label{sec:limits}
\paragraph{Limitations.} These are search results, not optimality proofs, and tightening is a local method. Each instance had 32 runs per method at a fixed budget; the $n=12$ cube record came from one of 192 runs, and ``only one method reached it'' means only one in 32 runs. Gradient paths are matched in steps, not pair evaluations or wall-clock time; the baselines are matched to a hardening run's pair evaluations, which perturbation--compression overshoots (Table~\ref{tab:diag}). Simulated annealing and perturbation--compression are our implementations; their best runs were less often close enough to be tightened, so the tightening policy favours the gradient paths, most for pc. The lowest-point tuning used instances larger than the test set, and in that round every winner sat on a grid edge or corner and, as pre-registered, the grid was not extended. In 3D only the three gradient paths were compared. Catalogue values come from captions and witnesses as of October 2026; truncated values are only known to their printed precision. Not detecting a difference (between mixed and single-path portfolios, or between snap and harden on most families) is not evidence of equivalence at these sample sizes.

\paragraph{Open directions.}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
\item \emph{Choosing the path per instance.} The family pattern suggests a rule: harden when good packings tilt some pieces against an otherwise regular arrangement (squares in squares), start rigid when the piece's inscribed disk is a poor proxy for it (triangles). A cheap pilot of both paths, then a budget split by early results, is the natural test.
\item \emph{Other paths.} Paths through other shape families (an ellipse for a rectangle, a rounded octahedron for a cube, superellipses \cite{Jiao2008}), paths that harden pieces one at a time, or schedules that adapt $\tau$ to the current overlap.
\item \emph{Larger $n$, other solids, other solvers.} Most unsolved instances have $n\ge17$; tetrahedra and octahedra in a cube were not attempted. Hardening could also seed exact models \cite{Berthold2026} or shrink-and-separate nesters \cite{Gardeyn2025}.
\item \emph{Non-convex and mixed pieces.} The rounded-shape construction needs a convex core; non-convex pieces would need a different family of intermediate shapes, for example a convex decomposition hardened part by part.
\end{itemize}
