\section{A wider test}\label{sec:breadth}
\paragraph{Design.} Seven families: unit squares in a square, a circle and a triangle; unit triangles in a triangle and a square; unit hexagons in a square; and, as a control, unit-diameter disks in a square, where hardening changes nothing because a disk is already a disk. Best known values come from the Squares Project \cite{squaresproject}, Friedman's catalogue \cite{friedmancenter} and Packomania \cite{packomania}; truncated catalogue values (``$2.956+$'') count as reached within their printed precision. All $n$ from 2 to 30 (to 20 for hexagons) are used except five reserved for tuning, \CsevenInstances{} instances in all.

Each method was tuned twice, first by median gap on held-out instances (C01--C03; a $3\times3$ grid, extended past its edge by a fixed rule, so harden, sa and pc saw 27 settings and grow and rigid 18), then by the lowest size reached (C06; one $3\times3$ grid per method). The second round used larger instances ($n>30$) than the test set, so its settings may be less suited to small $n$; Table~\ref{tab:range} breaks the results down by $n$. Each method then ran 32 times per instance at the matched budget of Section~\ref{sec:method}, and in each instance and method the best run (if within 2\% of the best known value) and any run within $2\cdot10^{-3}$ of it, at most three, were tightened. Every campaign's plan, including its decision rule, was committed before its first run; changes after a campaign started are recorded as amendments.

\paragraph{Result.} Table~\ref{tab:main} counts, per family, the instances on which each method's lowest size is the lowest of all five. Rigid starts reach the lowest size most often overall (\CsevenRigidLowest{} instances, against \CsevenGrowLowest{} for grow and \CsevenHardenLowest{} for harden) and reach the best known value most often (\CsevenRigidSolved{} against \CsevenGrowSolved{} and \CsevenHardenSolved{}). Resampling each instance's 32 runs with replacement (1000 times) gives 95\% intervals of \BootRigid{} for rigid, \BootHarden{} for harden and \BootGrow{} for grow, \BootSa{} for sa and \BootPc{} for pc: rigid's lead over the other two gradient paths is about the width of the intervals, and harden and grow cannot be told apart. Over instances solved by exactly one of the two, harden against rigid is \CsevenSignRigid{} (exact sign test, $p=\CsevenSignPRigid$). The pre-registered tests were one-sided; we report two-sided $p$-values throughout, which are at most twice the one-sided ones. Simulated annealing improved most from tuning for the lowest point; perturbation--compression stays far behind at this budget, partly because its best run was within reach of tightening on only \EligPc{} of instances (against \EligHarden{} to \EligRigid{} for the gradient paths; Table~\ref{tab:diag}). No run went below a published value, and \CsevenUnsolved{} instances were solved by no method. The median-tuned comparison (C04, Table~\ref{tab:median}) also puts rigid starts first.

\paragraph{How often does a method get there?} A count of instances hides how close each call was. Figure~\ref{fig:heatmap} shows every instance: most instances are ties; squares in a square is the only family where every instance but one that differs favours hardening, triangles lean towards rigid starts, and squares in a circle alternate. Figure~\ref{fig:bestofk} turns the 32 runs per method into the chance that $k$ random runs include one that reaches the best known value. Hardening reaches Trump's $n=11$ and Wainwright's $n=19$ packings in 2 and 3 of 32 runs, and rigid starts in none; on triangle instances the picture is reversed. Statements below that one method is ``the only one'' to reach a packing are statements about 32 runs.
